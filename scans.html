Brain imaging · Magnetic resonance imaging

A real brain scan, slice by slice

This is an MRI of the human brain that you can cut in any direction. It is not one person. It is the MNI ICBM152 template, made by aligning the scans of 152 adults and averaging them. Brain imaging researchers use it as a shared map, so a position given in millimetres means the same place in every study.

Measured, averaged Real MRI scans from 152 people, averaged into one brain. The skull has been removed and the brightness rescaled for display.

Reading a T1 scan

MRI can be tuned to show different tissue properties. This scan is T1-weighted, the kind used most often to show anatomy. Tissue that recovers its signal quickly between radio pulses looks bright, and tissue that recovers slowly looks dark.

White matterBrightest. Bundles of nerve fibres wrapped in fatty myelin.
Grey matterMid-grey. Cell bodies of neurons, on the surface and in deep nuclei.
FluidDarkest. Cerebrospinal fluid in the ventricles and around the brain.

Turn on Show tissue colours to see the same three classes painted in. Each colour marks voxels (3D pixels, 1 mm on a side) where the template's own tissue maps give that tissue a probability of at least one half. Voxels where no tissue reaches one half are left unpainted. They sit mostly at boundaries.

How MRI makes the picture

Line up

The scanner's magnet is tens of thousands of times stronger than Earth's field. Hydrogen nuclei in the body's water and fat behave like tiny magnets, and slightly more of them line up with the field than against it.

Tip and listen

A radio pulse tips them out of line. As they relax back, they give off a faint radio signal. How fast they relax depends on the tissue around them. That difference is the contrast you see.

Find the place

Extra magnetic fields that change across the scanner, called gradients, make the signal's frequency depend on position. A computer turns those frequencies back into an image, slice by slice. MRI uses no X-rays.

Why it looks smoother than a real brain

The template was built in rounds. In each round every one of the 152 scans was warped to fit the average from the round before, and a new average was taken. The result is much sharper than a plain average, but no two people fold their cortex the same way, so the smallest folds still blur. Large features such as the ventricles, the deep nuclei and the main folds line up well across people and stay sharp.

The numbers beside the viewer are in MNI space, the coordinate system this template defines. Zero is near the anterior commissure, a small bundle of fibres that crosses the midline. Positive numbers mean right, front and up.

Limits

This is an average, not a person. It has no lesions, no individual folding pattern and no age-related changes. Real scans vary a great deal.

The skull was removed with the brain mask that comes with the template. Brightness was rescaled for display, so the grey levels are not the scanner's raw values.

Tissue colours come from probability maps, not from looking at cells. Grey matter in the deep nuclei and the brainstem is partly mixed with white matter and is easy to misclassify.

This page is for learning anatomy. It cannot be used to read or diagnose anyone's scan. Only a radiologist or doctor can interpret a medical image.
