Sleep · Memory

One night of sleep, stage by stage

Sleep is not one state. Through the night the brain moves through distinct stages, each with its own electrical signature. Below is a real night, recorded at home and scored by sleep experts thirty seconds at a time. After it comes what the evidence says, and does not say, about sleep and memory.

Measured A real overnight recording from PhysioNet's Sleep-EDF database, with the experts' own stage labels. Filtered to remove slow drift and noise.

The night

The night comes from the Sleep-EDF Expanded database on PhysioNet. Its home recordings were made in 1987 to 1991 by healthy volunteers taking no sleep medication. All were described as white, so the group is not diverse. Technicians scored each night by eye, one 30-second page (an epoch) at a time, using the 1968 Rechtschaffen and Kales rules. Those rules have stages 1 to 4 and REM.1,2 This is one person's night. Nights differ between people and from night to night.

Old rules and new rules

In 2007 the American Academy of Sleep Medicine published new rules with five stages: W, N1, N2, N3 and R. Old stages 3 and 4 were merged into one deep-sleep stage, N3, because the task force found no evidence for splitting deep sleep in two. 'Movement time' was dropped. Four years after the new rules came out, the merger was among the changes critics cited most often.2,3

The two rule sets give similar but not identical results. When nights from 72 adults were scored both ways, the new rules gave about 9 more minutes of deep sleep, 11 more of N1 and 20 fewer of N2. Total sleep time did not change.4

Experts disagree too

In an AASM program, more than 2,500 scorers each staged the same 1,800 epochs. On average a scorer matched the most popular answer about 83% of the time. Agreement was lowest for N1 (63%) and N3 (67%) and highest for REM. Telling N2 from N3 was especially hard.5

Under the old rules used for this night, scoring was least consistent for stage 1 and only moderately consistent for deep sleep, so the labels above carry some uncertainty.2

Cycles through the night

Sleep runs in cycles of non-REM followed by REM. In lab recordings pooled from 369 healthy people, the typical cycle lasted about 96 minutes. It varied a lot from person to person, and the first cycle was usually shorter.6

In this night, 173 of the 220 deep-sleep epochs (79%) came in the first half of the time asleep. REM was split more evenly: 67 of its 125 epochs (54%) came in the second half.

Electrodes and age

These recordings used a forehead-to-crown lead and a back-of-head lead, not the usual lead from the centre of the scalp to behind the ear. A test in 10 patients found that switching leads changed the scoring about as much as switching between two human scorers, with a slight tendency to score sleep as deeper.7

Sleep also changes with age. Across 65 studies of 3,577 healthy people, the share of deep sleep and REM tends to fall through adulthood, while light sleep and waking in the night rise. How large the changes were depended a lot on how carefully people were screened for health problems.8

Sleep and memory: what the evidence says

Research on sleep and memory goes back about a century. One of the first studies comparing forgetting during sleep and waking was published in 1924.9 Below, each finding is labelled by how well it holds up. Some well-known results have shrunk or failed to repeat, and they are listed here too.

Holds up, smaller than first thought

Sleep after learning helps, modestly

On average, people remember more after a period of sleep than after the same time awake. A meta-analysis pooled 271 groups from 177 studies published from 1967 to 2019 and found a moderate advantage for sleep. After correcting for studies with weak results going unpublished, the advantage shrank to small, but did not disappear.10

It was larger when people studied the material more than once, and after a night of sleep than after a daytime nap. These patterns come from comparing different studies, so they are clues, not proof.10 Afternoon naps also showed a small-to-medium benefit across 54 studies.11

Theory, partly tested

Why it helps is still debated

An older idea is that sleep simply shields new memories from new information. A newer one, 'active systems consolidation', says that during deep sleep the hippocampus replays recent experiences, and sleep rhythms steer that information into networks in the cortex, often keeping the gist rather than every detail.12,13

In people with epilepsy who had electrodes placed in the hippocampus for their treatment, recordings show the rhythms nested in time: spindles during the 'up' phase of slow waves, and fast ripples in the troughs of spindles. That shows the timing exists. It does not, on its own, show that the timing improves memory.14

Causal, in rats and mice

Rodent experiments show cause

In rats, disrupting hippocampal ripples after training made spatial memory worse.15 Timed pulses during sleep that strengthened the coupling of ripples with cortical slow waves and spindles improved next-day memory.16 In mice, triggering spindles in step with slow waves improved memory, and suppressing them made it worse.17

These experiments are strong evidence that the rhythms matter, mostly for spatial memory, in rodents.

Contested

Do spindles predict memory in people?

A 2023 meta-analysis of 53 studies found a small-to-moderate link between spindles and memory, stronger for skills than for facts.18 A reanalysis argued that once publication bias is corrected for, the link disappears.19

A 2025 meta-analysis of 23 studies found that tighter timing between slow waves and spindles goes along with better retention.20 Spindle activity also tracks general thinking ability across people, so some links may reflect stable traits rather than overnight memory work.21 All of these are associations, not proof of cause.

Did not hold up reliably

Boosting slow waves with sound

In 2013, sounds timed to the 'up' phase of sleepers' slow waves strengthened the waves and improved memory for word pairs in a small study.22 A double-blind repeat with 19 people boosted the waves but not memory.23

Across 11 experiments, the memory benefit was small and not statistically significant.24 Another meta-analysis found the reported benefit shrank steadily with year of publication.25

Small but reliable on average

Cueing memories during sleep

In targeted memory reactivation, a sound or smell present during learning is given again during sleep, gently enough not to wake the sleeper. Odour cues during deep sleep improved memory in 2007,26 and sound cues strengthened single memories in 2009.27

A 2020 meta-analysis of 91 experiments with about 2,000 people found a small average benefit for cueing in non-REM sleep, and no clear benefit for cueing in REM sleep or while awake.28 Several studies found no benefit, for example in 11 to 13 year olds learning vocabulary and in adults over 60.29,30,31 For now it is a research tool, not a proven way to study.

Contested

Is REM sleep needed for memory?

A 2001 review judged the evidence weak and contradictory. It noted that people whose REM sleep is suppressed by drugs or brain injury do not show memory deficits.32 In one lab study, antidepressants that cut REM after learning did not harm memory for skills or word pairs.33 Another antidepressant impaired one kind of visual skill learning, so the picture is mixed.34

On the other side, quieting a REM-sleep brain rhythm in mice after learning impaired their memory, while quieting it outside REM did not.35 Meta-analyses found no overall extra benefit of sleep for emotional over neutral memories.36,37

Contested

Skills: an open question

A meta-analysis of finger-sequence learning found large gains after sleep at first glance. Once testing artifacts were taken into account, it found no evidence that sleep improves the skill.38 Other researchers challenged parts of that analysis, and the authors replied that their result held.39,40

Fairly consistent, likely overstated

Losing sleep before learning

People kept awake all night showed less hippocampal activity while learning and remembered less later.41 Across studies from 1970 to 2020, a night without sleep before learning was linked to a medium-sized drop in memory, and missing sleep after learning to a smaller one. Signs of publication bias and small studies mean the true effects may be smaller.42

Partial sleep loss matters too: sleeping 3 to 6.5 hours, compared with 7 to 11, caused a small drop in forming new memories across 39 reports.43

Bottom line. Most evidence suggests sleep helps memory to some degree, but the effects look smaller, more dependent on the task and less tied to deep sleep than early studies suggested. Sleep-memory researchers themselves have called for more repeat studies and higher standards.44 Whether REM sleep in particular is needed is still debated. This page is educational and is not advice about sleep problems. If you have trouble sleeping, talk to a doctor.
