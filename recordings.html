Electrophysiology · EEG and ECG

Reading the body's electrical signals

Neurons and heart cells both work by moving charged ions across their membranes. When millions of cells do it together, the voltage change is large enough to pick up through the skin. Electroencephalography (EEG) records it from the scalp. Electrocardiography (ECG) records it from the chest. Everything below is a real recording from PhysioNet, a free public archive of research recordings.

EEG: the alpha rhythm

In 1929 Hans Berger reported a rhythm of about ten waves a second over the back of the head. It grows when a person relaxes with their eyes closed and shrinks when they open them. It is now called the alpha rhythm, and it is still the first thing people learn to see in an EEG.

What the electrode picks up

Not single spikes. An EEG electrode sums the slow currents from synapses on millions of pyramidal neurons in the cortex below. They line up in parallel, so their currents add up. Only when many fire in rhythm does the signal grow this large.

Why closing the eyes changes it

With the eyes closed, little visual information arrives, and large groups of neurons in the visual cortex and thalamus fall into a shared rhythm near 10 Hz. Opening the eyes breaks it up into smaller, faster, less regular activity. Studies that compare the two states find the largest alpha change over the back of the head.

What EEG cannot do

Its timing is precise to milliseconds, but it is poor at saying where a signal comes from. The skull spreads the current out, and deep structures contribute little. For location, researchers use MRI or recordings inside the brain.

ECG: reading a heartbeat

Each heartbeat starts in a small patch of tissue in the right atrium, the sinoatrial node. An electrical wave spreads from there across the atria, pauses briefly, then races through the ventricles. Each part of that journey leaves its own wave on the ECG.

Same physics as a neuron

Heart muscle cells fire action potentials like the one on the Spike page, but much longer. Each cell triggers its neighbours through direct electrical connections, so the wave sweeps across the whole heart.

Early beats

Switch to Early beats. In this recording some beats start in the ventricles instead of the sinoatrial node. They are called premature ventricular complexes. They come early, have no P wave in front, and are wide and oddly shaped, because the wave spreads slowly through muscle instead of the fast wiring. A longer gap often follows.

What a single lead shows

A clinical ECG uses twelve views (leads) of the same heartbeat. These recordings use one, a modified lead II, which runs roughly along the heart's main axis and shows the P wave well.

Not a self-check. These are textbook examples labelled by experts, not a way to judge your own heart or brain. Early beats are common and often harmless in healthy people, but sometimes they matter. Only a doctor who knows the person can say which. If you have symptoms such as fainting, chest pain or a racing heart, seek medical care.

Data and sources

Recordings: EEG Motor Movement/Imagery Dataset (subject 1, runs 1 and 2, electrode Oz; spectrum from O1, Oz and O2) and MIT-BIH Arrhythmia Database (records 100 and 119, lead MLII), both from PhysioNet under the Open Data Commons Attribution License v1.0. Processing: zero-phase band-pass filters (EEG 1–40 Hz, ECG 0.5–40 Hz). Spectra use Welch's method with 2-second windows. The averaged heartbeat lines up every normal beat of record 100 that has a normal beat on either side, and each wave's peak was found by searching a time window around it. The script is tools/recordings/prep_recordings.py in this site's repository.
