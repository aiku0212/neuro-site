Model · Medial temporal lobe

How a brain could keep a compressed timeline of the past

Simulation A computational model with chosen parameters. The cells shown are not recordings.

0.0 smodel time

Population activity, last 45 s

Rows are cells sorted by preferred delay (short at the bottom, log-spaced). After each event a sweep of firing climbs the rows and slows down: that curve is the signature of a logarithmically compressed timeline.

What the population says: when?

Each bump is the whole time-cell population's estimate of when one event happened, on a log axis. The bumps slide left and slow down but keep the same width: recent past is sharp, distant past is blurry in seconds but equally sharp in ratio.

What you are looking at

The top sheet is a population of Laplace cells, the kind reported in lateral entorhinal cortex. Each one jumps when an event happens and then decays exponentially at its own rate. Left to right, they go from fast decay to slow decay. On their own they do not say when anything happened; together they hold the Laplace transform of the recent past.

The curved band below is a population of time cells, the kind reported in hippocampus. Each fires at a preferred delay after an event, and cells tuned to longer delays fire later and for longer. Each time cell reads only a few neighbouring Laplace cells (the thin wires), which is all the math needs. Four rows, one per event type, because many time cells respond to a particular stimulus.

Present a few events, then watch the raster and the timeline below. Push k up and every field gets sharper; the timeline stays log-compressed at every setting.

The two equations

Laplace cells, one per decay rate sdFs/dt = −s Fs + f(t)
Time cells, Post's inverse at s = k / τ*f̃(τ*) = ((−1)k / k!) · sk+1 · dkF / dsk

After a single event τ seconds ago the time cell with preferred delay τ* responds as a gamma curve that peaks exactly at τ* and whose width grows in proportion to τ*. Every cell has the same coefficient of variation, 1/√(k+1). With τ* spaced logarithmically, that is Weber–Fechner compression of the past: the same number of cells for 1 to 2 seconds ago as for 10 to 20.

The model predicts that judging which of two events came first depends on the ratio of their gap to their age, not on the gap in seconds.

That prediction is testable. In simulation, order-judgment accuracy curves for events 2, 8 and 32 seconds old sit far apart when plotted against the raw gap and fall onto one curve when plotted against gap divided by age.

Status

This is a model, not data. Its parameters are illustrative, and nothing here has been fit to recordings yet.
