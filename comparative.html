Comparative Neuropsychiatry

Dogs have the same drug receptors as people but cannot describe symptoms

Everything else on this site measures plasticity (the brain's ability to change) in humans, the one species that can describe what a drug feels like. Dogs are prescribed the same drugs, which act on the same receptors, but they cannot describe anything. That gap is not a limitation of veterinary medicine. It is a central open problem in animal neuroscience, and parts of it are measurable.

If your pet takes one of these medications

This page is research commentary, not veterinary advice. Adverse-event reports cannot show that a drug caused a problem, or how often it happens. Do not stop or change a pet's medication, or give a pet a medication prescribed for a person, without talking to a veterinarian. About and limits

Drugs given to both people and dogs

The “What each intervention acts on at the synapse” section of the Evidence page models fluoxetine binding weakly to TrkB, a receptor whose signalling helps neurons grow and strengthen their connections. It also models ketamine reducing inhibition in the local network. Both of those drugs are given to dogs. So are most of the others.

Gabapentin adverse-event reports in people and dogs

The percentages are measured. The interpretation below them is a hypothesis. The figures compare the reactions reported with gabapentin in dogs and in humans. They come from the FDA's two spontaneous-reporting systems, which collect reports of suspected reactions as they arrive rather than through planned studies. The data were retrieved from openFDA, the FDA's public data service, in November 2026. Dog figures count safety reports only: 8,444 of 9,028. Almost all of the 584 left out are reports that a product did not work. Gabapentin is rarely the main product in these dog reports. In the first 100 safety reports openFDA returns, it was never the first drug listed. The first drug was usually another veterinary drug, such as bedinvetmab, grapiprant or carprofen. So the dog figures almost always describe reports in which gabapentin was given alongside something else, not reactions to gabapentin alone. The human figures also count gabapentin in any role.

Measured Percentages come from openFDA report counts. The converging lines show the hypothesis, not data.

Taken at face value, the numbers suggest dogs are more than five times as likely as people to become lethargic on gabapentin. (The human figure pools two terms: 0.59% of reports say lethargy and 2.71% say somnolence, 3.21% with either. Treating them as one symptom is a judgment call, and using lethargy alone would make the gap far larger.) That reading is probably wrong, and the reason is the main problem this page is about. About a fifth of human gabapentin reports list at least one of six internal states that are hard to see from outside: pain, fatigue, nausea, headache, dizziness or anxiety. Owners can record some of these: pain appears in 5.64% of dog reports. A dog given the same drug, which acts on the same receptors, may have its experience recorded as a single word, because that may be the only word the observer has. Differences in patient populations and in reasons for prescribing also contribute.

Canine "lethargy" may not be one symptom. It may be many different inner symptoms, all recorded as the one sign a person can see from across the room.

The same problem extends beyond pharmacovigilance, the monitoring of drug safety. Supervised models that recognize pain in animals learn from examples that people have labelled. The labels are one of two kinds: an expert's reading of the animal's behaviour, or whether the recording was made during a stimulus expected to be painful. Neither is the animal's own report, because the patient cannot be interviewed. Models of emotional states such as anxiety or distress face the same gap. A 2024 review of automated pain recognition calls the lack of a consistent ground truth (a reliable correct answer to train and test models against) a significant limitation (Chiavaccini et al., Frontiers in Veterinary Science 2024). That limitation is the problem this work is meant to address.

Three bridges from this site to pet psychiatry

Each connects an existing section of this site to a pet behaviour or condition that has a public data source. They are ordered by how directly the site already covers the mechanism. These are not yet sourced. Treat them as hypotheses, as explained under Provenance below.

Bridge 01 · Critical periods

Unsourced hypothesis

The socialization window

The Evidence page argues that critical periods, times early in life when experience changes brain circuits most easily, are actively closed by molecules that limit how much the brain can change. It uses songbirds to show that the timing of that closure differs by species. Dogs show the same pattern, with lifelong effects on behaviour. A puppy's socialization window closes in the first months. After that, fear responses generalize, spreading to similar situations, and rarely fully reverse.

Bridge 02 · Compulsion

Unsourced hypothesis

Canine compulsive disorder

Researchers treat tail-chasing and acral lick dermatitis (sores from repeated licking of a leg or paw) as a naturally occurring model of human OCD. These behaviours respond to the same drug class, and their concentration in certain breeds implies a genetic basis rather than environment alone. They form a spontaneous animal model. The behaviours arise on their own in homes shared with people, something no rodent model offers.

Bridge 03 · Decline

Mostly unsourced

Cognitive dysfunction

Canine cognitive dysfunction syndrome involves amyloid pathology, a build-up of amyloid protein in the brain. It shows up as disorientation, altered social interaction, and sleep-cycle disruption. Dogs live shorter lives and are exposed to the same indoor environments as we are, so a longitudinal cohort (a group followed over time) covers adulthood to old age in years rather than decades. The dog-to-human age relationship is not a fixed ratio: a DNA methylation study in Labrador Retrievers found it is non-linear (Wang et al., Cell Systems 2020). DNA methylation is a pattern of chemical tags on DNA, and parts of the pattern change with age.

Not yet covered on this site · Dog Aging Project is the public cohort

Provenance

The four counts at the top and the gabapentin figures are computed and can be reproduced from openFDA count queries. They carry the limitations documented alongside the analysis. Spontaneous reports have no denominator, meaning no count of how many animals or people took the drug. Disproportionality, where a reaction makes up a larger share of one group's reports than another's, is not a measure of risk. The note that gabapentin is rarely the first drug listed comes from records retrieved in openFDA's default order, not from a count query.

The bridge sections are not yet sourced. They are hypotheses written from background knowledge, with at most one citation each, and have not been checked against current literature. Treat them as directions for research, not as evidence, and please do not cite them.
