Membrane · Hodgkin and Huxley, 1952

A neuron does not send a signal. It lets one happen.

The membrane sits at rest holding a voltage it paid for with ATP. Push it past about -55 mV and sodium channels open faster than the membrane can resist, which opens more of them. That runaway is the spike. Everything below runs the real 1952 equations in your browser, integrated live, so the trace responds to what you change rather than replaying a picture.

1. RestLeak and the pump hold about -65 mV. The h gate sits open, waiting, which is the part people forget.
2. Upstrokem opens in tenths of a millisecond. Sodium enters, depolarizing further, opening more m. Positive feedback with no brake yet.
3. Repolarizationh closes and n opens, both slower than m. Sodium shuts off while potassium leaves. The membrane falls.

Three experiments that make the point

Each one runs the same equations with one thing changed. They are the classic demonstrations because they are the ones that falsify the obvious intuition.

All or none

Six stimulus strengths straddling the model's threshold, about 13.3 uA/cm² for a half millisecond pulse. Below it the membrane sags back. Above it every spike is the same height: 35 uA/cm² buys a peak of 40 mV where 13.5 buys about 36. A stronger push does not make a bigger spike, it makes an earlier one. Intensity is carried by rate, not amplitude.

Refractory period

Two identical pulses, separated by 8, 14, 17 and 22 ms. The second is blocked outright until about 17 ms, because the h gate has not released its inactivation yet. Then it comes back at full height. There is no half sized spike anywhere in between, which is the all or none rule seen from a second angle. This sets the ceiling on firing rate, and it is why a spike cannot run backwards down the axon.

Temperature

The same axon at 6.3, 10, 14 and 17 C. Rates scale by a Q10 of 3, so warming makes the spike faster and smaller: the peak falls from 38 mV to 29 mV between 6.3 and 14 C. At 17 C this stimulus stops firing at all. Potassium now activates fast enough to cancel sodium before it can run away. That is heat block, and it appeared in these equations before anyone cornered it at the bench.

What is actually being integrated

Four coupled differential equations, stepped forward in time. The membrane is a capacitor with three leaky resistors across it, and two of those resistors change their own resistance depending on the voltage they see.

C dV/dt = I - gNa m³h (V - E_Na) - gK n⁴ (V - E_K) - gL (V - E_L) dm/dt = αm(V)(1 - m) - βm(V) m // opens fast, closes fast dh/dt = αh(V)(1 - h) - βh(V) h // closes slow, the brake dn/dt = αn(V)(1 - n) - βn(V) n // opens slow, the repolarizer C = 1 µF/cm² E_Na = +50 mV E_K = -77 mV E_L = -54.4 mV forward Euler, dt = 0.01 ms, rates scaled by 3^((T - 6.3)/10)

The exponent on m is the whole trick. Three gates must open together for one channel to pass current, so conductance rises as the cube of a probability. That is what makes the upstroke steeper than anything else in the cell.
