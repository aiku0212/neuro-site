How a neuron fires an action potential

At rest, the membrane holds a voltage that the cell maintains by spending ATP. Raise the voltage past about -55 mV and sodium channels open faster than the membrane can return to rest. Sodium flows in, which raises the voltage further and opens more sodium channels. That runaway feedback is the spike. Everything below runs the original 1952 equations in your browser. 1. RestPotassium, sodium and leak currents balance at about -65 mV. The model has no pump. It treats the ion gradients as fixed. The h gate, which later shuts the sodium channels, sits mostly open at rest. This is the part people forget.
2. UpstrokeThe m gate opens in tenths of a millisecond. Sodium flows in and makes the inside even less negative (depolarizes it further), which opens more m gates. This is positive feedback, and the slower gates that end it have not acted yet.
3. RepolarizationThe h gate closes and the n gate opens, both more slowly than m. The sodium current shuts off while potassium flows out. The membrane voltage falls.
4. Undershoot (after-hyperpolarization)The n gates are still open when the voltage gets back to rest, so the membrane drops below rest toward E_K, the voltage at which potassium has no net flow, before settling. The refractory period overlaps this dip but is not the same thing. It starts earlier, during the spike itself.

Three classic experiments in the model

Each one runs the same equations with one thing changed. They are the classic demonstrations because each one disproves what most people would first expect.

All or none

The chart shows six stimulus strengths on either side of the model's threshold, which is about 13.3 uA/cm² for a half millisecond pulse. Below threshold the membrane returns to rest. Above it, every pulse gives a full spike of nearly the same height. A 35 uA/cm² pulse gives a peak of about 41 mV, and a 13.5 uA/cm² pulse gives about 36 mV. A much stronger stimulus adds only a few millivolts. Mostly it makes the spike come earlier. Neurons signal intensity by how often they fire, not by spike height.

Refractory period

The chart shows two identical pulses, separated by 8, 14, 17 and 22 ms. In this model the second pulse fails until the gap reaches about 15 ms. Near that limit the cause is potassium. Its channels are still slightly open, and the membrane is still about 3 mV below rest. At shorter gaps, sodium channels are also still recovering from inactivation (the h gate). By 17 ms the second spike is back at full height. No gap in between gives a half sized spike. This is the all or none rule again, seen in a second experiment. The block is not total, though. A much stronger second pulse, 80 uA/cm², fires a slightly smaller spike at an 11 ms gap. The refractory period sets the upper limit on firing rate. It is also why a spike cannot travel backward along the axon.

Temperature

The chart shows the same axon at 6.3, 10, 14 and 17 C. The gate rates have a Q10 of 3, which means they triple for every 10 C of warming. Warming therefore makes the spike faster and smaller. The peak falls from 38 mV to 29 mV between 6.3 and 14 C. At 17 C this 15 uA/cm² stimulus no longer triggers a spike. Potassium channels now open, and sodium channels inactivate, fast enough to stop the runaway feedback. This is a raised threshold, not heat block. In heat block the axon stops firing altogether. Here the threshold has gone from about 13.3 to about 15.3 uA/cm², and a slightly stronger pulse still fires. Real squid axons keep firing at much higher temperatures than this. Hodgkin and Katz (1949) found a reversible heat block between 35 and 40 C.

The equations the simulator solves

The simulator steps four linked differential equations forward in time. The model treats the membrane as a capacitor with three resistors across it, one each for sodium, potassium and leak current. The sodium and potassium resistors change their conductance (how easily current passes through them) with the membrane voltage.

C dV/dt = I - gNa m³h (V - E_Na) - gK n⁴ (V - E_K) - gL (V - E_L) dm/dt = αm(V)(1 - m) - βm(V) m // opens fast, closes fast dh/dt = αh(V)(1 - h) - βh(V) h // closes slowly, shuts off sodium dn/dt = αn(V)(1 - n) - βn(V) n // opens slowly, repolarizes C = 1 µF/cm² E_Na = +50 mV E_K = -77 mV E_L = -54.4 mV forward Euler, dt = 0.01 ms, rates scaled by 3^((T - 6.3)/10)

The exponent on m is the key detail. Three gates must open together for one channel to pass current, so conductance rises as the cube of a probability. That is what makes the upstroke steeper than anything else in the cell.
