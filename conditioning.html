Learning and behavior · Classical and operant conditioning

How conditioning works, and why extinction does not simply erase it

Conditioning is learning that one thing predicts another. In classical conditioning a cue comes to predict an event, such as a tone that comes before food. In operant conditioning an action comes to predict a consequence, such as a lever press that brings food. The bench below runs a classic learning rule, the Rescorla-Wagner model, through training, extinction and a test. Then it shows where that rule breaks.

Why the plain rule fails the tests

With the plain rule the tone carries a single number, its associative strength V, which stands for how strongly the tone predicts food. Training pushes V up toward a ceiling. Extinction pushes the same number back down. Once V is near zero, nothing is left to recover. Test in the same room, a new room or after a delay and the answer is the same, because rooms and time are not part of this version of the model.

Animals do not behave that way. After extinction the response comes back when the cue is tested outside the place where extinction happened. This is called renewal. It happens when the animal returns to the training room (ABA renewal, named for the rooms used in training, extinction and test). It also happens in a room the animal has never seen (ABC), and when training and extinction share a room and only the test moves (AAB). ABC and AAB renewal are generally thought to be weaker than ABA. The response also returns when time passes after extinction. That is spontaneous recovery, first reported by Pavlov. A third relapse, reinstatement, happens when the US is given again on its own in the test room.

Bouton and colleagues read these results as evidence that extinction does not destroy the first memory. It adds a second, inhibitory memory, roughly that the tone no longer predicts food, and that second memory works mainly in the context where it was learned. The original learning, by contrast, often transfers well to new rooms. On this view spontaneous recovery is renewal in time. The extinction memory was learned at one moment and the test comes at another.

Switch on the context-gated memory to see this idea at work. The model now keeps two numbers. E is the excitatory memory from training and I is the inhibitory memory from extinction. The response is E minus I, and I counts fully only in the room where it was learned and only soon after. How much it is turned down elsewhere is hand-set. Nothing in this toy says how a brain does the gating.

The plain rule deserves one fair note. Rescorla and Wagner treated the room itself as a second cue that is always present, so a training room could gain its own link to the US and an extinction room could become an inhibitor. That version can produce some ABA renewal. When researchers tested for those room associations directly, though, they often found none, even where renewal was strong.

Conditioning tracks prediction, not just pairing

A tone can be followed by shock many times and still teach an animal no fear of it. In 1968 Robert Rescorla ran fear-conditioning experiments in which tones and shocks came with set probabilities. When shock was just as likely without the tone as with it, the tone produced no conditioned fear, even though tone and shock were often paired. Fear of the tone grew as shock became more likely during the tone and shrank as shock became more likely without it. Rescorla's name for the key quantity is contingency, which means how much more likely the US is with the cue than without it. The contingency idea also predicts the reverse. When the US is less likely with the cue than without it, the result is inhibitory conditioning, and the cue comes to work against what other cues predict.

Twenty years later Rescorla summed up the change in the field. The old description, one stimulus taking over another's response because the two were paired, did not hold up. Conditioning is now described as learning the relations among events, so that an animal can represent its world.

Blocking makes the same point from another side. If a tone already predicts food, then pairing a tone plus a light with food teaches very little about the light. It is known as the Kamin blocking effect. The Rescorla-Wagner rule explains it because learning on each trial depends on the gap between what happened and what all the cues present already predicted. The food is no surprise, so there is little error to correct. Waelti, Dickinson and Schultz used a blocking procedure in monkeys. Both the animals' learning and the responses of their dopamine neurons followed this prediction error, not the bare pairing of a cue with reward.

The part in brackets is the prediction error. When the outcome matches what the cues already predict, nothing is learned. Rescorla describes the model as error correction and notes that it is essentially the same as the Widrow-Hoff learning rule, which closely matches the delta rule used in many artificial neural networks. It is a formal description of when conditioning happens. It is not a description of neurons.

Learning from the consequences of actions

In operant conditioning, also called instrumental conditioning, the animal's own action is what predicts the outcome. A rat presses a lever, a food pellet arrives, and pressing becomes more frequent. If presses stop producing food, pressing declines. That is operant extinction, and it also depends on new inhibitory learning that shows relapse effects such as renewal.

Two pairs of words sort the consequences. Reinforcement means the behavior becomes more likely. Punishment means it becomes less likely. Positive means a stimulus is added. Negative means a stimulus is taken away. None of the four words means good or bad.

Positive reinforcementAdded, behavior goes up A dog gets attention for sitting when it greets people, so it sits more.
Negative reinforcementRemoved, behavior goes up A water spray stops when the dog stands on all four paws, so standing becomes more likely.
Positive punishmentAdded, behavior goes down A water spray when the dog jumps up makes jumping less likely.
Negative punishmentRemoved, behavior goes down Attention is withdrawn when the dog jumps up, so jumping becomes less likely.

Illustrative Examples follow those given by Casey and colleagues for dog training.

Negative reinforcement is not punishment. Both can involve something unpleasant, but negative reinforcement makes a behavior more likely, because the behavior ends the unpleasant thing. Casey and colleagues point out that one event often does both jobs. The same spray punishes jumping and, when it stops, reinforces standing.

The positive and negative labels are less tidy than they look. Adding something always means it was absent before, and removing something means it was present before. Baron and Galizio could find no new evidence that lets researchers reliably sort a reinforcer into one kind or the other.

The words matter outside the lab. In a matched-pair study, dogs whose owners reported using two or more aversive methods (positive punishment or negative reinforcement) were slower than dogs trained only with reward-based methods to reach ambiguous locations in a judgment task. The difference was statistically significant for two of the three ambiguous locations, and the authors read it as a sign of a more negative mood. The study was correlational, so it cannot show that the training caused the difference. The Comparative page looks at psychiatric drugs given to both people and dogs.

Extinction in the brain

Brain recordings show signs of the extinction memory. Mohammed Milad and Gregory Quirk recorded neurons in the infralimbic cortex, part of the medial prefrontal cortex, in rats that had learned to fear a tone and then had that fear extinguished. These neurons fired to the tone only when the rats were recalling extinction the next day, and the rats that froze least showed the largest increase. Pairing the tone with brief stimulation of this area lowered freezing even in rats that had never gone through extinction.

A review by Bouton, Maren and McNally describes Pavlovian extinction as depending on a circuit of the amygdala, the prefrontal cortex and the hippocampus. Prefrontal inhibition of amygdala neurons that encode the fear memory is involved in recalling extinction, and circuits linking the hippocampus and prefrontal cortex are involved in relapse, including renewal.

Cues, rooms and drug tolerance

Context shapes drug effects too. Siegel and colleagues proposed that cues tied to drug use trigger conditioned responses that anticipate the drug and add to tolerance. They offered this as one explanation for some heroin "overdose" deaths in people. Their animal experiments suggested that these drug-anticipating responses, in addition to the drug's own pharmacology, affect how many animals die from heroin. The Pharmacology page shows how major drug classes act at the synapse.

Habituation, learning to ignore

Habituation is a non-associative form of learning. It involves one repeated stimulus, not a relation between two. A response shrinks when the same stimulus repeats, and the drop is not caused by tired senses or tired muscles. Researchers rule those out by showing that the response still appears to a different stimulus, or that a different stimulus restores the response to the original one, which is called dishabituation. Like an extinguished response, a habituated response recovers at least partly when the stimulus is withheld for a while.

In the sea slug Aplysia, Castellucci, Kandel and colleagues traced habituation of the gill-withdrawal reflex to the direct connections between sensory neurons and a motor neuron. Working in an isolated ganglion attached to a patch of skin, they found that repeated stimulation weakened the direct synapses from sensory neurons onto a motor neuron, and that a strong stimulus to another pathway strengthened them again. Changes in those specific synapses could explain habituation and dishabituation in part, and perhaps entirely.

What this model leaves out

The Rescorla-Wagner rule has known gaps. Its author gives two examples. It fails to predict the conditions under which an inhibitor is extinguished, and it ignores detailed timing between events. The Time page models how the medial temporal lobe could keep a timeline of the past.

The context-gated version here is a cartoon of Bouton's idea, not his full account and not a circuit. Its gate values are hand-set. It leaves out reinstatement and rapid reacquisition, the quick return of the response when the cue is paired with the US again, so the bench does not offer training again after extinction. This page also does not cover reinforcement schedules.
